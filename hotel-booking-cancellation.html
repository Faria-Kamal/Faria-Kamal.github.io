CHAPTER 01/ CASE STUDY · MACHINE LEARNING

Hotel Booking Cancellation Prediction

SCENE 01 — A MODEL AND ITS LIMITS

A hotel loses revenue when a guest cancels and the room stays empty. This project asks whether booking details available at reservation time can help identify likely cancellations — and how much a model can actually be trusted.

Dataset
119,390 bookings · 32 columns
Methods
4 classifiers + K-Means
Context

The question

I worked on two problems with one dataset. First, a supervised task: predict whether a booking will be cancelled, comparing four classifiers against a sensible baseline. Second, an unsupervised task: group similar bookings with K-Means to see whether cancellation patterns differ across groups.

The important constraint was honesty. A hotel would not use this model in isolation — it could support planning, but the results had to be presented with their limits.

Data and preparation

The data covers two hotels (a city hotel and a resort hotel) with arrivals from July 2015 to August 2017. Each row is one booking. I removed reservation status and its date because they reveal the final outcome and would leak information into the model. I also excluded fields that could change after booking, since they would not be known at prediction time.

Cleaning removed identical rows and four rows with missing child counts; missing countries were labelled "Unknown". Negative values and bookings with no guests or nights were removed. I kept zero and unusually high daily rates because they may be genuine and I did not want to silently reshape the data. This left 86,634 rows.

Why this matters: every exclusion has a stated reason. "Tidy numbers" were never the goal — a defensible analysis was.

Approach

I structured the notebook around five classes so each stage had one responsibility:

  • DataLoader — reads the CSV and exposes the dataframe.
  • DataAnalyzer — inspects types, missing values, duplicates and plots exploratory charts.
  • DataPreprocessor — cleans rows, engineers features, encodes categories and splits the data.
  • ModelBuilder — trains and compares models, tunes a Random Forest, and evaluates on held-out test data.
  • BookingClusters — groups bookings with K-Means and summarises cancellation rates per group.

Feature engineering added total guests, a children/babies indicator, weekend share and a log-transformed daily rate. Scaling was learned from training data only, and the test set was used once, for final evaluation — not for model selection.

K-Means produced four booking groups with cancellation rates from 15.48% to 36.67%. That suggests booking patterns relate to cancellation behaviour, but it does not prove why guests cancel.

Read this honestly: the model correctly flagged many cancellations, but it missed 2,827 actual cancellations and wrongly flagged 720 completed bookings. Accuracy alone would hide that.

Limitations and what I learned

  • Only historical data was used, so performance on future bookings is unknown.
  • Some bookings may belong to the same guest, but guest identity is not available.
  • The model showed signs of overfitting: 89.05% training score versus 79.53% test score.
  • Recall is too low to rely on alone — next steps would test cost-sensitive thresholds and validate on newer data.

This project taught me to set a baseline first, keep the test set untouched until the end, and report the metric that matches the business cost rather than the one that looks best on a slide.
